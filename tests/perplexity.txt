Bread is one of the oldest prepared foods. Archaeologists have found charred crumbs at sites that are more than fourteen thousand years old, long before people began to farm grain on purpose. The earliest breads were flat: a paste of crushed seeds and water, spread on a hot stone and baked until it held together. Raised bread came later, probably by accident, when a batch of dough was left out long enough for wild yeast from the air to settle in it and start to feed.

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. When it eats the sugars in flour, it releases carbon dioxide and a small amount of alcohol. In a stiff dough, the gas cannot escape. Instead it collects in tiny pockets, and the dough slowly swells. The structure that traps the gas comes from gluten, a network of proteins that forms when wheat flour is mixed with water and kneaded. Kneading stretches and aligns the proteins into long, elastic strands, which is why a well-kneaded dough springs back when you press it with a finger.

Baking turns this soft foam into something solid. As the loaf heats up, the gas in each pocket expands and the yeast makes one last burst of carbon dioxide before the heat kills it. Bakers call this oven spring. At around sixty degrees Celsius the starch in the flour absorbs water and swells, and a little later the gluten sets, fixing the shape of the crumb. The surface dries out first and reaches the highest temperature, so sugars and proteins there react with each other to form the brown crust and most of the smell that fills a kitchen when bread is baking.

A basic loaf needs only four ingredients: flour, water, salt and yeast. The proportions matter more than most people expect. A common starting point is five hundred grams of flour, three hundred and fifty grams of water, ten grams of salt and five grams of dried yeast. Bakers usually describe recipes in percentages of the flour weight, so this dough has seventy percent hydration. Wetter doughs, at eighty percent or more, produce an open crumb with large, irregular holes, but they are sticky and harder to shape. Drier doughs are easier to handle and give a tighter, more even texture, which suits sandwich bread.

Time is the ingredient that is easiest to overlook. A dough that rises quickly in a warm room will taste mostly of yeast. The same dough left overnight in a refrigerator rises slowly, and during those hours enzymes in the flour break starch down into simpler sugars and bacteria produce acids that add flavor. Sourdough takes this idea further. Instead of commercial yeast, it uses a starter: a mixture of flour and water that has been fed regularly for days or years until it supports a stable community of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. The bacteria give sourdough its tang, and the acid they produce also helps the bread stay fresh for longer.

Even simple bread rewards practice. The first few loaves are often dense or flat, usually because the dough did not rise long enough or the oven was not hot enough. A baker learns to judge readiness by feel rather than by the clock: a properly proofed dough holds a gentle dent when pressed, instead of springing back at once or collapsing. Many home bakers keep notes on each attempt, recording the temperature of the kitchen, how long each stage took and how the loaf turned out. Over a few weeks those notes become a personal recipe that works in their own oven, with their own flour, better than any instructions copied from a book.
